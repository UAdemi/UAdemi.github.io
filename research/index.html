Research

How can we evaluate a policy when the policy changes the system through which its effects spread?

My work sits at the intersection of causal inference, network analysis, and comparative politics. I am interested in settings where an intervention does not simply act on a fixed structure, but reshapes it — and in what credible evaluation looks like once that is true.

Dissertation

Causal inference when interventions reshape the network

Most methods for estimating treatment effects assume that the relationships through which effects propagate remain stable. My dissertation takes the opposite case as the object of study: an intervention changes behavior and changes the network of relationships through which behavior spreads.

The aim is to make credible evaluation possible when interventions, relationships, and behavior all move together over time — estimating effects along a trajectory rather than at a single fixed point.

Three essays:

  • Identification under responsive networks. What can be identified when the treatment alters the structure through which effects travel.
  • Estimation on a single network over time. Recovering effects from one network observed repeatedly, rather than many independent units.
  • Empirical application. Taking the method to observed political networks and asking what it shows that fixed-network methods miss.

Status: in progress, Northwestern University · Political Science, with an M.S. in Applied Statistics.

Publications

Democratic transition and party polarization: a fuzzy regression discontinuity design approach

Ademi, U., & Kimya, F. (2023). Party Politics.

The question. Does democratization itself make party systems more polarized, or does polarization simply continue a trend already underway?

The approach. A fuzzy regression discontinuity design isolates the causal effect of transition from the pre-existing trajectory — a quasi-experimental strategy for a question that cannot be randomized.

Data

Nationalist elites network data

An original relational dataset covering 2,019 documented interactions among 606 political elites across three late-Ottoman nationalist movements — Albanian, Kurdish, and Macedonian/Bulgarian — spanning thirty years.

No comparable dataset existed, so the records were assembled and coded by hand from primary and secondary archival sources in five languages: Turkish, Ottoman Turkish, English, Albanian, Bulgarian, and French.

Available on request for academic use.
