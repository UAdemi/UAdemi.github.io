About

I have always loved solving difficult problems — especially when finding a better answer can make a real difference.

I am drawn to situations where the information is incomplete, the system is changing, and a decision still has to be made. My instinct is to bring structure to the uncertainty: clarify the real question, determine what the evidence can support, and turn the analysis into something people can act on.

What connects my work

That motivation has taken me across research, data consulting, entrepreneurship, and public communication. The settings are different, but the work often begins in the same place: an important problem without a ready-made playbook.

Research taught me to define a question precisely enough that evidence can answer it. Statistics taught me to be honest about uncertainty. Entrepreneurship taught me that analysis eventually has to become a decision — and that someone must take responsibility for what follows. Consulting reinforced the importance of beginning with the decision itself, rather than with the most sophisticated method available. Teaching and public communication taught me that an answer no one can understand is not yet a useful answer.

Together, these experiences shaped how I work: analytically, collaboratively, and with a strong bias toward useful outcomes.

What I am working on now

I currently work at the intersection of data, decisions, and changing systems.

At Northwestern University, I am completing a Ph.D. in Political Science and hold an M.S. in Applied Statistics. My dissertation develops causal methods for evaluating decisions that change not only outcomes, but also the systems through which those outcomes unfold.

I also work with research teams on study design, complex data, statistical modeling, causal inference, and the communication of quantitative findings. Beyond academia, I have helped build and operate a consumer business and developed public-facing projects that made complex ideas accessible to broad audiences.

I am increasingly interested in another version of the same underlying problem: how people make consequential decisions when judgment is shared with AI. In particular, I am interested in when AI complements human expertise, when it produces overreliance, and how repeated interaction with intelligent systems changes trust, behavior, and institutions.

Where I come from

I studied Political Science and International Relations at Boğaziçi University in Istanbul, completing a B.A. with a double major in History and Dean’s High Honors, followed by an M.A. My graduate work there focused on elite networks in nationalist movements — which is where the dataset behind much of my later research began.

I am also a graduate of the Abdullah Tivnikli ISAR Foundation four-year program, which combines the classical study of Madrasah education with a rigorous Arabic language curriculum, and I studied Modern Standard Arabic at the Qasid Institute in Amman.

I am a native speaker of Turkish and Albanian, work professionally in English and Arabic, and read French and Ottoman Turkish. That range is not incidental to the research: the elite-network dataset could not have been built without it.
